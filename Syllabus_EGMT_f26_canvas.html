How do we know a claim is true?

This course is a hands-on workshop in making and testing arguments in the context of mathematics. We will generate conjectures from examples, search for counterexamples, and turn ideas into precise statements and proofs. Through problem-solving sessions and presentations, you'll practice evaluating arguments, giving and receiving constructive feedback, and communicating clearly in writing and speech. By experiencing mathematics as a creative process — where patterns suggest conjectures and logical reasoning turns intuition into conviction — you'll develop a practical sense for what counts as evidence in mathematics and theoretical sciences, and how to build reliable conclusions.

By the end of the course, you will be able to:

  • Define and delimit what constitutes valid mathematical evidence by distinguishing between examples, counterexamples, conjectures, and formal proofs, while recognizing the limitations of empirical observations.
  • Develop a framework for discerning different types of mathematical knowledge by exploring how empirical evidence, abstract reasoning, and logical structure work together to shape mathematical understanding.
  • Formulate and communicate mathematical reasoning by translating intuitive insights into precise statements, evaluating the soundness of arguments, and engaging in constructive dialogue to identify and resolve reasoning gaps.
  • Reflect on the nature of mathematical truth by examining personal assumptions about certainty, analyzing when and why certain arguments are conclusive, and articulating how purely empirical approaches can both inform and limit our understanding of complex phenomena.

How we work together in class

This is a hands-on, pen-and-paper course. Each topic moves from intuition to rigor, usually over one or two class meetings; the pace follows the class, not a fixed schedule.

Class activities

A topic typically moves through the activities below. Their order, length, and mix vary from meeting to meeting, depending on the topic and on how the class is progressing.

  • Primer: a short introduction to the core idea, so you have the tools you need before you start solving.
  • Problem solving: you work on a problem set in your home subgroup. Problems are roughly ordered by difficulty (Easy → Medium → Hard). I circulate, ask questions, and may invite nearby peers to listen in and challenge your reasoning.
  • Presentations: a student or a subgroup presents a solution, complete or partial, on the board. The rest of the class acts as "Skeptics": asking clarifying questions, testing the argument, and pointing out gaps. Some presentations serve as model solutions that show what good structure, clarity, and logical flow look like.
  • Acceptance: your subgroup aims to have its solutions "accepted" by the instructor. A problem is "accepted" when your subgroup can answer follow-ups and defend key steps without unresolved gaps.

Home subgroups (fixed)

On Week 2 we form "home subgroups" for in-class collaboration: δ (delta), θ (theta), ζ (zeta), ρ (rho), λ (lambda), φ (phi). Math and science use Greek symbols very often. Subgroups remain stable throughout the course.

Team roles (rotate each meeting)

In subgroups of 5–6, the following sample roles work well:

  • Explainer: articulates the current approach and restates the problem in your own words.
  • Skeptic: presses on assumptions, looks for gaps, and frames precise questions.
  • Counterexample Hunter: designs and tests examples/edge cases to probe the claim.
  • Recorder: maintains a clean write-up of the steps in the problem.
  • Verifier: checks computations and logic, and ensures steps follow from the stated problem.
  • Connector: ensures that all subgroup members understand and can explain the solution.

You may keep the roles informal or explicitly assign them in your subgroup, but make sure you rotate them at least each week, if not more often.

Commonplace Book weekly assignments

Each week you complete two assignments by hand in your Commonplace Book: a weekly reflection and one problem write-up. Submit clear photos or a single PDF scan of the pages to Gradescope (linked from Canvas) by Sunday 10:00 pm, each to its own assignment.

  1. Weekly reflection (300 words / 1 page limit) — question changes every week. Reflections are graded on a 10-point scale for thoughtfulness and engagement with ideas from class and your subgroup. Examples:
    1. What is the hardest mathematical fact I have ever seen and understood in my life? (first week assignment). Describe your mathematical journey in your own words (not just list math classes you took). What was the hardest mathematical thing you have ever seen and understood? This assignment will help me form balanced home subgroups starting in the second week.
    2. What counted as evidence for me? Name one math experience from this week and list exactly what made it convincing to you (e.g., a minimal example, a failed counterexample, a definition that removed ambiguity, an auxiliary statement that closed a gap). End with one thing that would change your mind about this experience being convincing.
    3. Reflect on the nature of mathematical truth. What does it mean for a mathematical claim to be true? How is it different from a scientific claim, like "water boils at 100 degrees Celsius"? What is the role of examples, counterexamples, and proofs in establishing mathematical truth?
    4. Assumption audit. Pick one problem from the problem set, and list all assumptions you used in your solution (not just those stated in the problem). For each, mark: needed / not needed / uncertain. Then try to drop one: either give a tiny counterexample that shows it was needed, or a brief note explaining why the argument still goes through.
  2. Problem write-up (1–2 pages). Choose exactly one problem marked ✏️ from the week's problem sets, and write a clear, self-contained solution for a classmate from another subgroup who has the handout but did not hear your table discussion. Your write-up must include:
    • Problem restatement: the problem in your own words, clarifying what is given and what must be shown.
    • Definitions and setup: notation and any terms you rely on.
    • Argument: your proof or solution, in logical sequence.
    • Clear conclusion: your final conclusion, stated explicitly.

    Write-ups are graded Meets (2) / Revise (1) / Missing (0). A Meets write-up is correct, logically complete, and easy to follow.

Week 1 exception: the first reflection, example (a) above, is due Thursday, October 22, 10:00 pm, so that I can form the home subgroups before Week 2. The first write-up is due Sunday, October 25.

Attendance

Consistent attendance and thoughtful in-class participation are absolutely essential for your successful completion of Engagements courses. Since each course is only 14 classes, each absence is significant. That said, there are situations in which you may need to miss a class meeting. In those situations, Engagements professors are asked to observe the following policy:

  • Excused absences: Authorized university activities (such as university-sponsored athletic events) and the observance of religious holidays are considered excused absences. However, you must submit a written request detailing the dates of these absences directly to me by email by October 27. If these types of absences would result in you missing more than 2 of the required class meetings, you may need to switch to a different course with fewer scheduling conflicts.
  • Other causes of absences: We all get sick and we all have experienced unexpected family and personal emergencies. If you must miss class for any reason, you should notify me in advance, when possible, and you are responsible for making up any missed work. In line with College policy, professors are under no obligation to offer alternative assignments or to organize and facilitate make-up work: students must take the initiative.

After the second absence, any subsequent unexcused absence will incur an automatic one letter-grade (or 10 out of 100 points) penalty. For example:

  • A student with "A" level work who misses a total of 3 classes would receive a "B" letter grade.
  • A student with "A" level work who misses a total of 4 classes would receive a "C" letter grade.

Important Reminder: If you are experiencing significant and pressing personal circumstances, particularly if those circumstances interfere with your ability to attend class and complete coursework, you should contact your academic advisor to talk through the situation as soon as possible. Help is available, and these situations can almost always be worked out — but you need to let me know that there's a problem.
